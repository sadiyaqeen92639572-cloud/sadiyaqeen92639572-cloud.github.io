Why the overnight percentage matters more than the schedule "label"

Parenting plans often get described in shorthand — "every other weekend," "2-2-3," "week on/week off." None of those labels tell a court or a child support formula what it actually needs: the precise number of overnights per year, converted to a percentage of the full 365-day year. Two schedules that sound similar can land on opposite sides of a state's shared-custody threshold once you count the actual nights.

That threshold matters because crossing it can change how child support is calculated — sometimes by applying a credit to the paying parent's obligation, sometimes by switching to an entirely different formula that factors in both parents' time and income together.

1

Count real overnights

Go through an actual calendar year (or the parenting plan's cycle) and count nights, not days.

2

Divide by 365

The percentage is overnights ÷ 365 × 100 — not overnights ÷ number of weekends or any shorter period.

3

Compare to your state's line

Some states use 25%, others ~35%, others a flat day count like 90, 100, or 121 nights.

4

Check the dollar impact

Crossing the threshold doesn't always mean the same reduction — the formula on the other side varies by state.

Need the actual dollar amount, not just the percentage?

This calculator tells you the overnight percentage. It doesn't tell you what that percentage is worth in your state, because every state applies a completely different formula on top of it — and most also combine it with a base income-shares or percentage-of-income calculation that this tool doesn't touch. A few real situations where that gap matters:

  • Negotiating a parenting plan before support is calculated — knowing that 3 nights every other week (≈21%) keeps you under Idaho's 25% line, while 4 nights every other week (≈28%) puts you over it, changes what you'd propose.
  • Checking a proposed order against the guideline — if your state applies a schedule/bracket formula plus a custody credit, you need both pieces to sanity-check a number a court or the other parent's attorney has proposed.
  • Modifying an existing order after a schedule change — moving from 80 to 130 overnights might cross two different thresholds in some states (a self-support reserve zone and a shared-custody zone) at once.
  • Comparing options across a state line — if either parent could relocate, the same overnight count can produce a materially different support amount depending on which state's guideline applies.
  • Sanity-checking your own math — before paying for a lawyer's worksheet, running the numbers yourself against the actual published formula catches obvious input errors early.
